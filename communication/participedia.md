## Title

REpublic – Draft Constitution for a Deliberative Republic (France)

## Brief description (280 characters max)

An anonymous citizens' initiative publishing an open draft constitution for France in which laws are adopted by randomly selected citizens' assemblies and executive power is appointed and overseen by them. The text is open to public comment.

## Narrative body

### Mission and Purpose

REpublic is an anonymous citizens' initiative that drafts and publishes a proposed constitution for France, called the "Deliberative Republic". The draft would replace the institutions of the Fifth Republic (1958) with a system in which laws are adopted by citizens' assemblies selected by lot, rather than by elected representatives. Its stated aim is to make sortition "the normal way sovereignty is exercised", while keeping executive power under continuous oversight.

### Origins and Development

The initiative presents its proposal as a response to four perceived weaknesses of the Fifth Republic: the concentration of power around the presidency, the weakening of Parliament (notably through Articles 16 and 49.3 of the 1958 Constitution), the limited social representativeness of elected bodies, and the reduction of citizen participation to periodic voting. It cites France's Citizens' Convention for Climate (2019–2020) as a precedent: 150 randomly selected citizens adopted 149 proposals, but many of the most significant ones were not implemented, which the authors interpret as evidence that consultative assemblies are insufficient.

The draft is published online. The version available in November 2026 (v26) contains 179 articles in 17 titles. It is published in French, with an English translation.

### Organizational Structure, Membership and Funding

The authors are not identified. The text is maintained in a public version-controlled repository; each change is proposed, discussed and recorded publicly. Information on funding is not published.

### Specializations, Methods and Tools

The draft constitution combines several deliberative mechanisms:

- **Thematic citizens' conventions** of 150 members selected by stratified lot, each dealing with one subject. They receive training, hold hearings with opposing experts and commission counter-expertise before adopting laws, then dissolve. Three rapporteurs follow up on each convention's work for five years.
- **An agenda-setting convention**, held every five years, which selects the subjects of the thematic conventions and sets budget priorities, and **an annual convention** that adopts the budget.
- **A Civic Agenda Authority** of 50 citizens selected by lot, which registers petitions and opens conventions. A petition signed by one per cent of the civic body obliges it to consider opening a dedicated convention.
- **An appointed executive:** there is no president or prime minister. Ministers form a college and are chosen by a 50-member appointments convention selected by lot, from public applicants who pass competence and integrity checks.
- **Oversight bodies**, including a citizens' plenary session that reviews the implementation of laws each year, a court of public integrity able to remove office-holders, and a Constitutional Council that reviews every law before it enters into force. A scientific council may also suspend a law; suspended laws return to citizens' conventions.
- **Territorial conventions** that run regions, departments, inter-municipal bodies and larger municipalities.
- **Safeguards:** a list of inviolable rights that cannot be restricted, even in a crisis; crisis measures affecting freedoms must be authorised by an emergency convention selected by lot in advance; constitutional amendments require a referendum.

Serving in a convention is a civic duty; members keep their full income and job protection, and expenses including childcare are covered.

### Major Projects and Events

The principal output is the draft text itself, published with explanatory pages, interactive diagrams, a frequently-asked-questions page addressing common objections to sortition, and a costed budget model. According to the authors' estimate, the proposed institutions would cost about €1.7 billion a year, compared with about €3.7 billion for the institutions they would replace. The figures are based on published French budget data and stated assumptions.

The authors propose a transition in which a law or referendum under the 1958 Constitution would create a constituent convention of 300 citizens selected by lot. A second convention of 150 citizens would draft a declaration of fundamental rights. Both texts would then be put to a ratification referendum.

### Analysis and Lessons Learned

The proposal has not been implemented and no independent evaluation has been published. Its design draws on practices documented for citizens' assemblies, such as stratified random selection, balanced expert input, facilitation and remuneration (see OECD, 2020). It goes further than most existing proposals by replacing elections entirely, a configuration that has not been tested at national scale. Questions raised in the literature on sortition apply directly to it, notably the legitimacy of decisions in the eyes of non-participants, the accountability of randomly selected decision-makers, and the influence of organisers and experts on deliberation.
